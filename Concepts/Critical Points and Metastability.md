### Critical points

The liquid-gas coexistence curve terminates at a **critical point**. Beyond it, there is no distinction between liquid and gas — a single **supercritical fluid** phase exists.

Why it terminates: as pressure increases, the gas phase is compressed, losing volume and entropy. Its energy also decreases as molecules are forced closer together and form more bonds. The free energy difference between liquid and gas shrinks until at the critical point it reaches zero — and so does every other physical difference between the two phases.

The solid-liquid coexistence curve has no critical point and extends forever. The reason is **symmetry** (Landau symmetry principle): the solid has a different symmetry group than the liquid (crystal lattice has translational/rotational symmetries; liquid does not). Two phases with different symmetry groups can never be continuously connected — there must always be an abrupt transition where symmetry is created or destroyed. Liquid and gas share the same (trivial) symmetry group, so a path exists that continuously deforms one into the other, going around the critical point.

### Metastable phases and ergodicity breaking

A **metastable phase** is a local — not global — minimum of free energy. Example: amorphous ice, formed by cooling liquid water too quickly for a crystal to nucleate. It has higher energy than crystalline ice but is rigid. Given enough time it converts, but "enough time" can exceed any practical timescale.

This is **ergodicity breaking**: the system is in equilibrium within a local free energy minimum, but cannot explore the full phase space on any accessible timescale. Time averages $
eq$ ensemble averages. The lower the temperature, the less energy available to cross the barrier, the longer it takes.

- Ergodicity breaking is a matter of timescale — in principle all systems are ergodic given infinite time (including quantum tunneling)
- In practice, metastable phases are thermodynamically stable for all purposes; amorphous ice is rare on Earth but believed to be the most common form of ice in interstellar space
